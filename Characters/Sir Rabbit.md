# Brandon & Sir Rabbit — the original self

> **One-line reading.** One wound at age seven sends *two* figures out of the grave: **Brandon**, the *Ballerina Boy* — the buried child, the stage-alive content that went into the ground — and **Sir Rabbit**, the searching self built over him, walking around identity-vacant but intact, looking for a name.

> [!quote] The frame this page turns on
> The wound doesn't only make a grave; it sends two figures out of it. **Ballerina Boy is the buried *content*** — the child himself, interred: the original expressive light, hidden after shame made visibility feel dangerous. He is not dead, but *preserved* underground. **Sir Rabbit is the searching *self*** that results — identity-vacant but intact, the one who says, in effect, *"I do not know who I am, but I must be someone,"* and goes looking. The full genealogy runs: **wound → buried child (Ballerina Boy) → searching rabbit (Sir Rabbit) → armor ([[Sir Lucius]]).** One injury, three downstream figures, each a different stage of the same unfinished sentence.

These two are kept on one page because they come from the same grave — but they are **deliberately distinct**, and the distinction is load-bearing. Ballerina Boy is the *child* who was buried; Sir Rabbit is the *self* built over him. They also sit at different confidence tiers, and this page keeps them apart: **Brandon is literal, triple-confirmed autobiography**; **Sir Rabbit is a mythic/dream figure read as interpretation.** Read the two sections below as two figures sharing an origin, not one figure with two names.

## Brandon — the buried child (Ballerina Boy)
*[grounded autobiography]*

> **One-line reading.** **Brandon**, the *Ballerina Boy*, is the buried child — the stage-joyful, undefended self that went into the ground at seven, and the one the whole arc is trying to dig back up.

In the language of the inner system, he is **the exile** — the most vulnerable, most feeling part, sealed away because being seen once cost too much. Everything else the arc builds — the protector, the searcher, the grown interpreter — is organized around the fact of him being down there. Keep that tenderness in view: this is the sincere wound at the center, the thing the machinery was built to guard, not a bit to riff on. The central question is not whether he existed, but whether the system can become safe enough for him to return.

### At a glance

- **Role** — the origin/light layer of the [[Jagwar Twin]] system, and its **exile**: the wounded, feeling child every other figure is a response to.
- **Start with** — the song [[Ballerina Boy]] and [[Roy]]'s page, where this figure is the founding injury.
- **Core conflict** — he was not just hurt; he was told his very being was wrong, which forced [[Roy]] to bury him alive and build [[Sir Lucius]] over the grave.
- **Key symbols** — [[The Moon]] (light that survives the dark) · the stage (where he came alive) · the dance (the buried, feminine-coded joy).
- **Confidence** — grounded autobiography (the wound is real and publicly told) braided with strong interpretation (his role as the system's origin).

### Why this figure matters

Most of the Jagwar Twin cast are masks, voices, or faculties. Ballerina Boy is a person — specifically, the seven-year-old [[Roy]] used to be, before the world convinced him there was something wrong with being him. He is the *content* every other figure is built to protect, hide, weaponize, or rescue. [[Sir Lucius]] is the armor; [[Roy]] is the grown man deciding whether to go back for him; [[Mary]] is the voice that blesses him. Ballerina Boy is the thing all that machinery is *about*.

He's also the rare figure here whose backstory is not mythology. The wound is literal autobiography, told publicly by [[Roy]] outside the lyric, and triple-confirmed: in the song itself, in a sit-down interview, and — independently — in a concert-venue artist bio that simply notes he *"embraced ballet as a child while his peers pursued sports."* When the work feels heaviest, it is usually because it has circled back to this kid.

And here is the quiet, devastating fact that the whole arc turns on: the child's own response to being shamed was not to cry it out and move on. It was to perform an execution. *"I killed that ballerina boy / I buried him inside."* A seven-year-old buried himself, and everything after — the masks, the protector, the depression, the search — grew out of that grave.

### The wound, in his own sequence

[[Roy]] released this story himself, in order, both inside the song and out of it. Age seven, a Christian school, ballet — *"I came alive when I was on that stage."* Then rumors; a teacher calls his father: *"We think your son is gay."* The boy overhears (*"I overheard cause he was mad"*) and reasons his way to the cruelest possible conclusion — *"Gay must mean bad or else they wouldn't call"* — and prays *"to God to make me good cause it wasn't my fault."* He is the only boy in the class; he stands *"back against the wall"* while the girls surround him with the chant that becomes the project's founding wound: *"you're not a real boy."*

In the interview telling, the picture is even more disarming: *"a little blonde bowl cut… tights, ballet shoes, tap shoes,"* the only boy in the room, who came home crying *"I'm never gonna dance again."* The comic tenderness of tiny tap-shoe Roy is exactly what makes the next part land — because the shame, he says, got *"pushed so deep into the subconscious you don't know where it came from."*

His father gives the one piece of love that's available: *"there's nothing wrong with you, my son."* It is real, and it is not enough — for reasons the song quietly tracks, and which the **Two blessings** reading below takes apart. The boy does the thing instead. He buries himself. *"I never danced again."*

### What he is, as a figure

Strip the biography down to a function, and Ballerina Boy is the **embodied, undefended, stage-alive self** — softness, play, feminine-coded expression, creativity that hasn't yet learned to protect itself. He is what a person is *before* they learn to perform a version of themselves that the world will stop punishing.

The wound is not that he was hurt. It's that he was told he was *bad* — at a Christian school, where social shame curdles into moral and spiritual shame. *"You're not a real boy"* doesn't critique what he did; it attacks what he *is*. That's the difference between an injury and a verdict, and it's why the response had to be so total. You can recover from being hurt. Being declared incompatible with survival is a different problem — and solving it is what forces the psyche to manufacture a protector.

### The grave is the origin of everything else

The load-bearing reading of the figure, and it reframes the entire cast: **[[Sir Lucius]] is not the ballerina boy, he's what grows around the grave.** When softness is buried as unsurvivable, the psyche still has to live, so it builds the opposite of softness: strategy, performance, control, mockery-before-being-mocked, power-before-vulnerability. That armor is Lucius, formed at the precise moment the child went into the ground.

Which means the relationship between them is gentler than it first looks. He is not *destroyed* by [[Sir Lucius]] so much as *hidden behind* him. Lucius is the guard at the door of the wound — severe, clever, mocking, often cruel, but originally built to keep the most vulnerable part from being shamed again. That reframes the whole quest. The arc is not *"defeat Lucius and free the boy."* It is: **earn enough trust that the protector no longer has to bury what he loves.** You do not storm the door. You make the room behind it safe enough that the guard can finally stand down — because the thing he is guarding was always the thing he loved most.

Two claims here, and they sit at different confidence levels so, worth keeping apart. That the burial *produced* Lucius is interpretation; the song never names him. But that Lucius is *invested* in keeping this grave closed is grounded fact, and it's the single most telling piece of evidence on the page. In a theatrical episode of the video series, Lucius mimes a little ballet move at the camera and sneers: *"Do you know what your lowest-performing song of all time is? Ballerina Boy. When you're vulnerable you LOSE!"*

That isn't random cruelty. A shadow who fact-checks your streaming numbers to wound you, the inner critic as a guy with a Spotify-for-Artists dashboard, is dark humor... but the comedy is doing serious work. Lucius mocks exactly *one* song by name, and it's this one. If [[Roy]] rescues the buried child, Lucius loses his monopoly on protection. A figure with a demonstrable stake in keeping a specific grave closed tells you what's buried there matters most.

> [!note]- Advanced reading — the same light, differently oriented
> The tightest single-sentence version of the genesis reading lives on the [[Weirdness]] essay: Lucius is *"the split-off radiance of the buried child — the same light that went underground at seven years old — operating under the wrong orientation."* That's the key move. Lucius isn't the *opposite* of Ballerina Boy; he's the **same light** bent. The child's stage-radiance, sealed off and re-tasked as armor, lit by fear instead of joy. It's also why [[Roy]] can say of the protector that *"the deal may have been worth it"* — Lucius *"genuinely held the buried light"* through years [[Roy]] couldn't. The protector kept the light alive by keeping it imprisoned. (See [[The Dweller on the Threshold]] for the mechanism by which a buried self accretes a guardian.)

### The loop — and why the answer isn't success

The structure of the song is the argument. Verse one is the schoolyard; verse two leaps forward to seventeen, when [[Roy]] *"started a band and I was on a mission"* — and the childhood chant survives the jump, transposed into the music industry. *"Driving 'round the country / couldn't afford an egg McMuffin / music just wasn't landing"* — and there it is again, the same verdict in a new uniform: *"you're not a real boy."* Then it escalates into the open existential question: *"What makes a real boy?"*

That egg McMuffin is the whole figure in one detail. A man stands at the most extreme fork imaginable — and the emblem of rock-bottom he reaches for is that he couldn't afford a fast-food breakfast. It's genuinely funny and genuinely devastating at once: cosmic stakes measured in hash browns. The smallness is the point. Humiliation scaled down to breakfast is still humiliation.

Same mechanism across every venue — schoolyard, religion, label, algorithm, audience, money: *sacrifice the true self so the outer world stops punishing you.* Ballerina Boy is where the exact logic [[Sir Lucius]] later weaponizes in [[Not Your Homie]] and [[Welcome to the Circus]] first appears — before it had a face.

Which is what makes the fork's resolution so pointed. *"Do I kill myself or come back for that kid I let go."* Notice the choice [[Roy]] does *not* offer himself: he never asks *do I become successful?* He asks *do I come back for that kid?* The chosen answer is **integration, not achievement** — it goes inward first, the exact inverse of a bargain song that says win the outer game and maybe feel something later. *"This song's for Ballerina boy / This song's a prayer for hope."* The song isn't a report of a rescue. It *is* the rescue act — a ritual object, a prayer with the kid's name in it.

But integration is not demolition, and this is the exile's hardest problem. The armor that buried him is the same armor that kept him — and the person — alive. So the whole arc can be compressed into a single question, and it is a tender one:

> [!quote] The master sentence
> *How can the buried light be allowed to flow without destroying the structure that kept it alive?*

That is why the answer can't be to win, and can't be to fight. Winning leaves the child underground; fighting the protector attacks the very thing that preserved the child through the years [[Roy]] couldn't hold him. The task is subtler and kinder — to let the light back up *through* the structure, until the structure no longer has to be a wall.

> [!note]- Advanced reading — the counter-algorithm, and the joke on Lucius
> The song refuses the performance economy on its own terms — it declines the metric. It risks being *"low-performing"* precisely because its worth isn't denominated in streams. Form and content agree all the way down: the off-album single about the off-album child, kept out of the very spectacle it diagnoses. (The kid who got told he didn't belong made a song that didn't make the tracklist — the structure rhymes so neatly it's almost a bit. *Why* it was kept off the album is not publicly stated, so that's a wink at the irony, not a claimed intent.) And the joke is on Lucius: he calls it the *"lowest-performing"* song, which is the entire point — it loses by the scoreboard because it refuses to play that game. He's wielding the one metric the song was built to ignore. See [[Communion vs Spectacle]] and [[The Audience]].

### The two blessings — and why the deepest one is feminine

The song answers the world's hostility not with armor but with blessing — and it does so twice, in two different voices that are easy to conflate and shouldn't be.

The **father's** blessing is narrated inside the story: *"there's nothing wrong with you, my son."* It's real love, and it fails anyway — because it's stated in the *negative*. It's a pardon that still operates inside the same right/wrong courtroom the teacher and the girls were using. It acquits the charge; it doesn't leave the court. Telling a shamed child *you are not broken* is simply not the same as telling him *you are, exactly as you are, good.*

The **outro** blessing is the other thing. Delivered by an older, motherly female voice, it's *generative* rather than exculpatory: *"You are one of a kind, God's design… so my son, let your light shine… The moon reminds us that even in the darkness there is light. There's no one else like you out there."* It doesn't answer an accusation; it claims the child's nature outright. (Both lines address *"my son,"* which makes them easy to hear as one speaker — but they're two voices, and the difference between them is the whole resolution.)

And the deepest blessing arrives in the feminine voice, **exactly where shame tried to erase him.** That placement is the song's quiet thesis: the divine feminine enters, in full, precisely at the site of the wound. Lucius protects by *armoring* — become untouchable. The maternal/divine voice protects by *blessing* — you are already, exactly, right. The arc hinges on that contrast.

> [!note]- Advanced reading — the moon, and the Mary-class voice
> The outro's *"the moon reminds us that even in the darkness there is light"* may be the most load-bearing symbolic line in the catalogue. The moon **does not generate light — it reflects it.** That's the signature of borrowed, preserved light: not a source, but proof a source exists somewhere past the dark. (See [[The Moon]], which lists this song as the *tender* pole of the symbol — *"preservation-through-darkness."*) The blessing voice is the clearest [[Mary]]-class appearance in the whole body of work, and possibly the earliest chronologically. The female, motherly voice is **grounded** (audio-confirmed); the identification of *that voice* with the named [[Mary]] of [[No. 1 Champion (la Conquistadora)|#1 Champion]] is the working assumption, held as a strong read until a casting credit settles it. Note: *who* sings the outro is not credited — present her as *"a motherly female voice,"* not a named performer.

### The figure in the nested system

The clearest way to hold Ballerina Boy is to place him at the center of the whole nested cast — because everyone else is defined by their relation to him:

- **Ballerina Boy** — the buried **original light**. The exile. The feeling, expressive self that went underground when it was made to feel dangerous.
- **[[Roy]]** — the conscious interpreter, the grown man trying to recover him: *"come back for that kid I let go."*
- **[[Sir Lucius]]** — the protector who hid him, and who guards the door so he can't be shamed again.
- **[[Mary]]** — the blessing that calls him out by name: *"let your light shine… you were born to shine."*
- **[[Jagwar Twin]]** — the container that holds all of them together.

And a distinction worth keeping straight: the **figure is bigger than the song**. The single *[[Ballerina Boy]]* is the *evidence* — the place the wound is stated outright and the name is first spoken — but the buried child it points to runs underneath the entire body of work, in songs that never say his name. Read the song to meet him; read the arc to understand why everything is organized around him.

Picture a rabbit driving down a highway at night, looking for something he can't name. That's the whole figure in one image: not a hero, not a villain, just a self in motion with no fixed identity yet — *"I do not know… but I must be someone."* Roy has put this rabbit on the record twice, and called him, in his own words, *"Sir Rabbit pre-Lucius."* That gloss is the section's whole thesis: the rabbit is the self *before* the protector-mind hardens into a guide.

What makes him worth a section of his own is timing. Every other figure in this world arrives *after* something has gone wrong — the mask, the mirror, the shame-voice. Sir Rabbit is the one snapshot we have of the self with the wound but without the armor: forgetful, searching, heart-recovering, and — crucially — still teachable. He is the moment before the second rabbit slides into the seat.

A small but load-bearing note up front: this is the **[[Roy]]-self at an earlier stage**, not a third character standing beside Roy and Lucius. In the grounded dream, *Roy* is the rabbit and *Lucius* is the **second** rabbit. So "pre-Lucius" means the self *before* the split — not a baby Lucius. Hold that and the rest follows.

### Why this figure matters

Most of the Jagwar Twin world is about what happens *after* the self has been intercepted — the mask that hardens, the mirror that traps, the voice that tells you you're a failure and is technically never wrong. Sir Rabbit is the rarest thing in the catalogue: a clean look at the self *before* any of that. He carries the wound but not yet the armor.

That's why his defining line lands the way it does. The Spider asks him point-blank, *"Who are you, Rabbit? And where does You come from?"* — and the rabbit answers, with total honesty, *"I do not know… but I must be someone."* That is the entire condition of the seeker in one couplet: empty of a fixed identity, but not empty of the conviction that there is a self to find. He is directionless and intact at the same time. The work is whether he gets a real guide before a fake one finds him first.

### The two rabbit texts

Roy has put the rabbit on the record in exactly two places, and the grounded claim is modest and clean: he pictures himself as a rabbit, across two texts. Reading that rabbit as *the seeker-self* — the Roy-pole before the split — is interpretation, but it sits very close to both texts.

**The poem (2022).** A long initiation poem Roy published on his own Hall of Mirrors platform — a rabbit in *"a garden full of sound"* who takes a walk to count the stars and gets pulled into a full cosmic journey: a strange cat, a violet moon that turns out to be a machine, a fox with a box, a forgotten heartbeat, a dove with a lamp, a grinning spider at the Divide, and a leap *"straight through the Mourning Sun"* that lands him *"back where he begun."* Roy filed it under origins (more on that below) and later glossed it himself: *"this looks sir rabbit pre-lucius."*

**The dream (2023).** Around the release of [[Good Time (1-08)]], Roy described a dream he'd had — and it's worth telling with the comedy intact, because the cosmic initiation literally begins as a celebrity bit. Roy leaves a party, a white minivan pulls up, and *"guess who's in the white minivan? It's Drake, naturally."* Drake gives him some fame advice (*"things I wish people would have told me when I started"*), then gets out and says *"it's your turn to drive."* The wheel changes hands and the dream proper begins. *"During this whole thing, I'm a rabbit. Somehow I know that."* He's driving, *"looking for something, but I didn't know what I was looking for,"* when a figure drops into the passenger seat — also a rabbit, *"but he's got like clown makeup on, and he says, I'm Sir Lucius… I'm here to help you."* The clown-makeup rabbit has all the answers and is factually right about every turn, *"but I had a weird feeling in my heart… he was telling the truth, but he was manipulating me in some way. And I couldn't explain why."* The dream ends in an involuntary crash — *"a white semi truck with a lightning bolt comes — boom, hits my car"* — and Roy wakes at 1:08. The title is the clock-time.

So the rabbit isn't a young Lucius. He's the self that exists *before* Lucius arrives, which is exactly why the counterfeit guide can intercept him.

### A strong reading: the authentic path and the intercepted path

Put the two texts side by side and the same journey is being told twice — once the right way, once hijacked. This is the section's central interpretive claim, and it's a strong one.

- **The poem is the authentic arc.** A real initiation, endured in order: the rabbit is carried up, trapped, forced inward, recovers his own heartbeat, is given light, is named at the threshold, and *chooses* the leap. He returns transformed — *"back where he begun,"* same starting point, different self.
- **The dream is the intercepted arc.** Same seeker, same directionless searching — but the *wrong guide arrives first.* Lucius takes the guide's seat before any real initiation has happened. He's factually right and emotionally wrong, and the rabbit's heart catches the manipulation before his mind can explain it. There's no chosen leap. There's a lightning-crash he didn't choose.

The cleanest way to hold the pair: **the poem shows the seeker's true initiation; the dream shows that same seeker intercepted at the threshold by the wrong guide.** One ends in a leap; the other ends in a wreck. (The crash, eerily, later showed up in waking life — a semi rear-ended Roy on the freeway. He noted he'd been listening to BTS right before, *"surely has nothing to do with the story."* Dry. And the crash is also a bit: the real truck driver climbs out, *first* worried about the wrong thing — *"oh no, I can't have TMZ coming here… you're a celebrity, right?"* Roy answers *"everyone's a celebrity,"* the driver squints at him and tries again — *"are you Russian?"* The initiation's most violent beat is delivered as deadpan.)

### The wound underneath

Sir Rabbit doesn't come from nowhere. He's what the childhood wound produces. The Ballerina Boy (Brandon, above) is the buried *content* — the embodied child told *"you're not a real boy"* and pushed down. Sir Rabbit is the searching *self* that results: identity-vacancy walking around, directionless but intact, still capable of taking a leap. And the armor that eventually forms over him is [[Sir Lucius]] / [[The Protector]].

The line runs: **wound → buried child → searching rabbit → armor.** Sir Rabbit is the third link — the one most easy to miss, because by the time anyone's looking, the armor's already on.

### The poem's small cast

The reason the poem matters beyond its prettiness is that it hands the world a *cast* that isn't reducible to Roy and Lucius. Each figure does one specific thing to the rabbit. Here's the scannable version:

| Figure | What happens | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| **The Cat** | A *"very strange Cat"* carries the rabbit up to the moon for lunch — they *"eat till half past two / With Seals and candles burning,"* the moon-machine's *"funny ears that always kept it clean"* | A psychopomp with a comic streak: the strange escort who lifts the seeker out of the ordinary world, then sits him down to a cosmic business lunch (candles, seals, and all) |
| **The Violet Moon** | Beautiful violet moon in the sky; on arrival it's *a machine* with *"whirling gears and funny ears"* | Mechanized reflection — charm first, apparatus revealed second (the exact shape of Lucius) |
| **The Fox** | Offers to teach *"The Game of Many Things,"* invites the rabbit into a box — the lid *slams shut* | A trickster who traps you… into yourself. Manipulative in form, medicinal in result |
| **The heartbeat** | Inside the box, the rabbit recovers a forgotten pulse — *"a heartbeat he forgot"* | The key to the *"other mind"*; the part of you the machine can't generate |
| **The Dove** | Waits in an emerald garden tree with a lamp, gives light, then *releases* the rabbit | A guide who helps and lets go — light without a leash |
| **The Spider** | Grinning, golden-caped, at *"The Divide"*; poses the name question, names the dreaming, offers the leap | The threshold-keeper; selfhood is a *name* problem before it's anything else |
| **The Mourning Sun** | The rabbit *chooses* to leap *"straight through the Mourning Sun"* and returns *"back where he begun"* | The passage home — transformed return, not a reset |

Three of these figures do enough work that they're worth pulling out of the table.

#### The Fox — not every trickster is Lucius

The Fox is the figure that keeps the cosmology honest. He's *"ever cunning,"* he baits the rabbit into a box and slams the lid — and then it turns out the box was the point. *"The key is hidden in yourself,"* he says. *"Just listen for the knock."* Trapped and sulking, the rabbit eventually hears the forgotten heartbeat behind the wall, knocks, and a door appears, unlocked. The Fox even arrives carrying **bread** — real provision a machine can't make.

So the Fox is **manipulative in form but medicinal in result.** Which means the world has a kind of cunning that *heals* — and that's load-bearing: **not every trickster is Lucius.** The contrast is exact. The Fox is manipulative-form / medicinal-result. Lucius is helpful-form / parasitic-result. One traps you into your own heartbeat; the other offers help and quietly feeds on you. Tell them apart by what they *produce*, not by how they behave on the way in.

#### The Dove — a Mary-style guide, not Mary

The Dove waits in an emerald garden tree, holds up a lamp so the rabbit can see — and then, gently, sends him on: *"As much as I love company you must be on your way."* That's help that doesn't trap you in devotion, which is the precise opposite of clinging Lucius. It reads as a [[Mary]]-*function* — a light-bearer who transmits and releases rather than catching and holding.

The caution matters here: this is a **Mary-register**, not the named Mary, and not "the clearest Mary in the catalogue." A guide who gives light and lets go *resembles* Mary's gesture without being her. Hold the resemblance; don't collapse it into an identity.

#### The Spider — selfhood as a name problem

At *"The Divide,"* a grinning, golden-caped Spider stops the rabbit with the question the whole figure turns on: *"Who are you, Rabbit? And where does You come from?"* The rabbit's honest *"I do not know… but I must be someone"* is the seeker's entire condition. The Spider's reply reframes everything — *"You are someone asleep in bed, you're dreaming all the time"* — and the rabbit snaps to: *"I must wake up… I must wake up tonight!"*

What makes the Spider a *true* guide is what he refuses to do. He names himself a guide (*"You're here at The Divide / I'm here to be your guide"*), he leads the rabbit to the edge — and then, asked *"is this a test? I'm not ready to fly!"*, he hands the choice back: *"that's up to you, it's not for my to pry."* He preserves the seeker's sovereignty. He won't make the leap *for* the rabbit. That refusal is exactly what the counterfeit guide will not do — Lucius answers everything *for* you.

### The Lucius mechanism this clarifies

Taken together, the rabbit texts pin down *how* Lucius actually works — and it's sharper than the better-known phrase "the truth that deceives." Lucius is the counterfeit guide who gives true answers **without the heartbeat.** He occupies the guide-seat *before* the initiation has been endured. The cleanest formulation:

> **Lucius doesn't only deceive by lying; he deceives by arriving too early with true answers.**

That's the engine underneath everything Lucius does. The poem shows what a real guide costs you — the box, the silence, the forgotten heartbeat you have to find yourself, the leap nobody can make for you. The dream shows the shortcut: a guide who skips all of it, hands you the correct turns, and lets your heart go quietly wrong while your mind nods along. Same destination words, opposite road. (This is *complementary* to the "truth that deceives" reading, not a replacement for it.)

> [!note]- Advanced reading — the "other mind" and the heart it's keyed to
> The poem doesn't just trap the rabbit; it tells him *how* to escape, and the instruction refines the heart/mind split the whole project runs on. A voice on the far side of the box says: *"Now you must use your other mind, dear Rabbit, it's been mapped."* The key to that other mind is the **heartbeat** — the rabbit waits in silence, hears *"a heartbeat he forgot,"* knocks over the spot, and the door opens.
>
> So the formula sharpens. A mind *severed* from the heart becomes Lucius. A mind *returned through* the heartbeat becomes the *"other mind."* This isn't heart *against* mind — it's mind *reoriented through the pulse it forgot.* And *"it's been mapped"* is doing quiet work: the faculty was always there. The rabbit isn't building a new mind; he's remembering one. (Call it anamnesis — knowledge by recollection.) The same seed-doctrine shows up in the Fox's line *"the key is hidden in yourself / just listen for the knock,"* which is Roy's later maze-password *"you are the key" / "you are it"* stated years early.

> [!note]- Advanced reading — the fox's box as the missing fourth container
> The world's broken-vessel logic ([[Keilim - The Breaking of the Vessels]]) usually sorts containers into a few kinds: the prison that seals the self away, and the husk that's form without seed. The Fox's box is a *third* thing — the missing **initiatory container.** Not a prison and not a husk, but an **interiorization chamber**: an enclosure that isolates the self just long enough to hear the forgotten heart, then opens.
>
> The catch is diagnostic. Boxes *look alike* from the outside — the lid slams shut on the rabbit exactly as a trap would. The difference isn't the enclosure; it's what the enclosure *produces.* Severance, or the heartbeat. That's the same test that separates the Fox from Lucius, scaled up to cosmology: you can't read a container by its shape, only by its fruit.

> [!note]- Advanced reading — Loop vs Circle, and the Mourning Sun
> The poem ends with the rabbit landing *"back where he begun"* — and that line is the sharpest statement of a distinction the work cares about a lot ([[The Circle]]). A **Loop** is return *without* transformation: the same point, the same self, nothing learned. A **Circle** is return *with memory restored*: the same point, but the subject has changed. Sir Rabbit returns to his starting place *after* the heartbeat, the Dove, the Spider, the Divide, and the chosen leap — so his return is a Circle, not a Loop. The geography is identical; the traveler isn't.
>
> One spelling flag worth keeping. The leap is through the *"Mourning Sun"* — transcribed as written from the artifact. If that spelling is intentional (and not a transcription slip), the ending reads as *grief-as-solar-passage* rather than a happy dawn, and the mourning/**morning** pun points straight at the **Morning Star** — Lucifer — Lucius. The rabbit passes *through* the morning sun and comes home. Spelling-dependent, so flagged, not asserted.

> [!note]- Advanced reading — the child-knight, the White Knight, and the maze cluster
> The poem travels with a small visual cluster found in the Hall of Mirrors. One open-leaf image is a photo of **young Roy** in toy plate-armor with a dragon shield, sitting on a pony. It reads as the *biographical genesis* of [[The Protector]] — the survival-self armoring up *before* it calcifies into Lucius. The careful version: the maze *selected a childhood-armor image that already holds the armoring structure.* It is **not** "Roy staged this as Lucius." The resonance is structural, not authored. (As the lab framing puts it: a child in a knight costume is also just a kid playing.)
>
> There's a literary rhyme here too. Carroll's **White Knight** — the protective-but-absurd guide who saves Alice and then insists on reciting "the name of the song" — chimes with both the toy knight *and* with the poem's proto-guides (Cat, Fox, Dove, Spider). It's a lineage note, not an authorial claim. And the poem itself is unusually well-sourced: it survives both on the crown-gated room pages *and* as three consecutive iPhone-Notes screenshots that are themselves open maze rooms, all matching the text verbatim — so it's triple-sourced, not single-witness.

> [!note]- Advanced reading — Roy's "silly rabbit" register and the food chain
> Outside the two canonical texts, Roy uses a whole comic rabbit-vocabulary for the Lucius-prone self. When online critics came for the project, he ran a deadpan food-chain bit: *"jagwars eat rabbits and theyre just being silly,"* *"rabbits are below jagwar on the food chain… its a hint for myself,"* and *"down the rabbit hole."* The trash-talk connects Sir Rabbit to [[The Jaguar]] along a predator/prey axis — but note how fast he folds himself in: *"We're all silly rabbits sometimes,"* and *"ill catch myself being a silly rabbit when i get mad."* Nobody's exempt, including him.
>
> After catching Lucius messing with him one week, he quoted the Trix cereal slogan at his own shadow: *"he totally tried to fuck me up this week but i caught him… silly rabbit, tricks are for kids."* His advice to a fan was to acknowledge Lucius and then *"put that silly rabbit back in the hat"* — the whole integration arc reframed as a magician's hat-trick. (Same night he glossed the poem as "pre-lucius," he also dropped *"I can pull a rabbit out of a hat"* alongside a *"Rudolph Steiner rabbit hole with the Gospel of Luke"* — the name-research and the rabbit imagery bundled into one evening. How the Steiner/Luke thread bears on the rabbit specifically is a documented research vein, not a stated equation.) The 2026 iconography grounds it further: *"The year begins with Janus and the March Hare,"* and a gematria aside resolving to a rabbit emoji.

> [!note]- Advanced reading — the maze provenance, Lemon, and the "earliest announcement"
> A few facts the casual reader doesn't need but the completist will want. The *Sir Rabbit* poem was filed by Roy in the maze's **GENESIS** collection — which holds exactly two origin pieces (the rabbit poem and *Song of the Ring*), so the rabbit is literally catalogued under *origins.* It was first solved via a spreadsheet cell whose column held a tiny rabbit picture; the solver simply *"tried the rabbit one,"* and Roy called the piece *"one of my favorite pieces i've ever made."*

That design is the collaborator's, **not Roy's**, so it's a resonance only, not part of the iconography canon.

### Source notes — Sir Rabbit

- **Grounded.** Roy pictures himself as a rabbit in two public texts: the *Sir Rabbit* poem (published 2022 on his own Hall of Mirrors platform, in the GENESIS/origin collection) and the [[Good Time (1-08)]] dream he narrated publicly (2023), in which he says *"I'm a rabbit"* and Lucius is the *second*, clown-makeup rabbit. Roy's own gloss — *"this looks sir rabbit pre-lucius"* — is the documented basis for reading the rabbit as the self *before* Lucius, and he dated the figure's debut himself: *"sir lucius first appeared in the video and artwork for good time 108."* The poem's text (the cat, the violet-moon machine, the fox's box, the recovered heartbeat, the dove, the spider at the Divide, the leap *"back where he begun"*) is quoted from the artifact's displayed pages. The Spider's companion maze-room (*"I'M THE SPIDER,"* code *"the answers in a name"*) is a solved, grounded artifact.
- **Strong reading.** That the rabbit *is* the seeker-pole — the [[Roy]]-self before the split — is interpretation, though it fits both texts closely. So is the central pair: the poem as the *authentic* initiation and the dream as the *intercepted* one. So is the Lucius mechanism it clarifies ("arriving too early with true answers"), the wound → buried child → searching rabbit lineage, and the Fox-vs-Lucius contrast.
- **Framework.** The Dove-as-Mary-function, the "other mind" keyed to the heartbeat, the fox's box as a fourth container-type, the Loop-vs-Circle reading, the Mourning/Morning-Star pun, and the child-knight/White-Knight resonances are interpretive frameworks laid over the material — folded into the Advanced reading callouts above and labelled as readings, not claims. The spelling of *"Mourning Sun"* is transcribed as-shown and flagged for verification.
- **Register guard.** This section deliberately does **not** say "Roy *is* Sir Rabbit," and does **not** treat Sir Rabbit as a third character peer to Roy, Lucius, and Mary. He's the Roy-register seeker at an earlier stage.
